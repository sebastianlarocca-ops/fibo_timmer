            How your session is picked

            The system knows nothing about muscles or strength. All it does is count what share of your training went to each movement pattern, and propose what's missing. Everything else is rules so that simple idea doesn't do something dumb.

            Push H Push V Pull Knee Hip

            Five patterns compete for the four non-core slots. Target is 20% each — that's just 1 ÷ 5, so it moves on its own if the list ever changes. Core sits out: it has its own block in every session.

            The seven steps

            They run in this order, each one using what the previous produced.

            1. Picks what to look at. Your last 12 sessions, dropping anything older than 60 days. It counts sessions, not calendar days — a two-week break would leave a "last 30 days" window deciding on three workouts.
            2. Counts patterns, not exercises. Every exercise adds a point to each pattern it trains. A burpee is horizontal push and knee, so it adds one to both. Those totals become your percentages.
            3. Scores each pattern. Two forces pulling in different directions — see below.
            4. Benches whatever you just trained. If your last session was two days ago or less, its patterns are out, however good their score. A hard veto, not a discount.
            5. Fills the four slots one at a time. After each pick it re-scores everything: that pattern's share goes up and its rest resets to zero, so it ranks lower next round. A cap of two slots per pattern keeps one from taking the whole session.
            6. Only then picks the actual exercise. Never-done ones first (max two new per session), then whatever you haven't done in longest. Everything from your last session is off the table.
            7. Explains itself. The sentences and bars you see are just the numbers above, written out. Nothing new is calculated there.

            The two forces

            65% How far behind

            How far under 20% it sits. A pattern right on target scores exactly half of this.

            35% How rested

            Days since you last trained it, over seven. It maxes out at seven days — resting ten or thirty is the same.

            That seven-day ceiling is what lets it handle breaks with no special rule. Training regularly, rest is what stops you repeating a pattern two days running. After a long break every pattern maxes out at once, rest stops telling them apart, and balance decides — which is exactly what matters when you come back.

            When the rules jam

            They can block each other. It loosens one at a time, in this order, and always says so on screen.

            • Window. Back from a long break with under three sessions in range, it ignores the 60-day limit and uses the last twelve however old.
            • Veto. If too few rested patterns are left to fill four slots, the benched ones come back — better than four exercises of the one pattern still standing.
            • Cap. Last resort, a pattern takes more than two slots. An empty slot is worse than a repeat.

            What it deliberately doesn't do

            • No randomness. Same data, same answer, every time — so you can always audit why it proposed what it did. Variety comes from the alternates listed under each exercise.
            • Doesn't look at load. Only two of your sessions have reps and weight logged. It counts appearances, not volume.
            • Doesn't measure intensity or fatigue. Its only notion of recovery is time since the last time.
            • Once is the same as three times. For rest, only the date counts. Volume enters through the window percentage, which moves slower.

            The limit worth knowing

            It can only propose what's in your catalog. Vertical push has two bodyweight exercises — hand stand hold and hand stand walk. Every time that pattern wins a bodyweight slot, it's one of those two. Pull and hip sit at three each. When a pattern keeps repeating the same exercise, that's the catalog talking, not the engine.
